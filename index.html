About Us

Goals and scope

We are a newsletter giving monthly updates on the latest mechanistic research into psychedelics, with a particular focus on psilocybin.


As a current student, I have often felt as though scientific research can be nearly impossible to understand without extensive education in an area. Psychedelics are and incredibly interesting and fast moving area of research, with a huge audience of people both inside and outside the field. However, the papers that talk about this research can be incredibly technical and hard to understand for those not working in the field. Our goal is therefore to act as a resource that people without a scientific background can use to easily understand, and stay up to date with the research.


We include a handful of papers each month that typically have at least some level of mechanistic insight at a cognitive neuroscience level of explanation. This means 2 things: Firstly, our newsletter is not an exhaustive list of the research that is published each month. Secondly, a huge amount of research outside the niche of cognitive neuroscience is not included (i.e., clinical, genetic, etc.) Additionally, because we try to simplify each paper to an accessible level, our summaries may include miscellaneous inaccuracies, or may omit certain details. We of course still try to represent the research as faithfully as possible, but these summaries should not be a replacement for reading the original papers.


Most papers on psychedelics will start by saying something along the lines of: "There is clinical data to support psychedelics as a novel treatment for certain psychiatric conditions". The prospect of psychedelics being a revolutionary new treatment across a variety of psychiatric conditions is incredibly exciting. However, we do not condone, recommend or support that any individual consumes these drugs in any recreational or self-medicating context.

October 2026

Volume 10 · 2 Papers ·

While much of the research on psilocybin focuses on the overall rate at which neurons fire, in this study researchers examine how acute psilocybin doses affect the burst dynamics of neurons (neurons firing in bursts of signals instead of overall consistent rate). Researchers recorded over 46,000 single neurons across the cortex, thalamus, striatum and hippocampus of head-fixed mice. Mice received saline, psilocybin, or ketanserin followed by psilocybin, to test which effects depend on 5-HT2A receptors.


The thalamus showed a bidirectional change in burst firing, with bursting increasing in the reticular nucleus, anterior thalamus and first-order somatomotor relay nuclei but decreasing in the geniculate nuclei. Given this, researchers suggest psilocybin shifts thalamic gating in a manner which may be responsible for rebalancing of bottom-up and top-down signalling. Ketanserin abolished the burst increases in the reticular, anterior and somatomotor nuclei, suggesting they depend at least partly on 5-HT2A receptors; however, the geniculate decrease could not be tested under ketanserin since this region was insufficiently sampled in those sessions, so its 5-HT2A dependence remains unknown.


In the hippocampus, psilocybin reduced the proportion of bursting neurons in CA1–CA3, most strongly in CA3, without changing firing rate. Again, ketanserin abolished this effect (in CA1 and CA3), suggesting this change is also mediated by 5-HT2A activation. CA3 neurons are naturally burst-prone and form recurrent loops involved in memory recall, so dampening their bursting may weaken the self-reinforcing activity that sustains maladaptive memories that are frequently prone in psychiatric disorders. Psilocybin also increased burst firing in the striatum, an effect likewise abolished by ketanserin.


Psilocybin's effects on firing rate were more restricted than its burst effects: the only change to survive statistical correction was a modest decrease in layer 5 of somatosensory cortex, which ketanserin did not block, suggesting a 5-HT2A independent mechanism. Notably, ketanserin on its own broadly suppressed firing rates across the brain, affecting both regular and fast spiking neurons, a previously unreported effect the authors attribute to the removal of tonic serotonergic drive.


Brain-wave recordings showed that psilocybin lowered high gamma power and, during rest, alpha power, alongside a theta increase. Ketanserin did not block these changes, suggesting they arise through a mechanism independent from the 5-HT2A-dependent burst changes.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.14.744865

Prior work from these researchers showed that the 5-HT1B receptor is needed for psilocybin’s anxiolytic effects in mice. However, it is unclear which populations of neurons matter for this effect. To test this, researchers genetically removed 5-HT1BRs from forebrain excitatory neurons. They gave mice a single dose of psilocybin, measured behaviour straight afterwards and again 48 to 96 hours later They also mapped brain activity two hours later to investigate how network level activity changed.


Mice without 5-HT1B receptors as well as mice with these receptors still both showed head twitch response (HTR) behaviours, suggesting 5-HT1B receptors did not affect the presence of a psychedelic experience. However, anxiolytic effects were considerably reduced. In control mice psilocybin increased behaviours that were associated with a lower state of anxiety, such as shortened the time to eat in a novel arena. These effects were absent or reduced in mice lacking this receptor, suggesting the excitatory neurons in the forebrain drive psilocybin’s anxiolytic effects.


At the network level, the receptor changed how psilocybin reorganised activity across the brain. In control mice, psilocybin cause activity across brain regions to become more desynchronised. In knockout mice, the opposite effect occurred, where synchrony increased. The authors suggest these receptors normally act as a brake that helps psilocybin decorrelate brain activity, and that without them, activity becomes more synchronised.


In knockout mice, increased synchronisation was concentrated in forebrain, thalamic and limbic circuits. Stronger responses in control mice were concentrated in brainstem circuits, including the pons and raphe nuclei. Researchers therefore suggest that these receptors dampen excitatory signals travelling from the cortex to the thalamus, producing decorrelation. In control mice, psilocybin also decoupled the amygdala from the hippocampus and midbrain, which they propose underlies the anxiolytic effect.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-026-07184-8

Psychedelic experiences depend heavily on setting. Many clinical trials deliberately manage the therapeutic environment, yet most brain wave studies of psilocybin measure people at rest. Researchers therefore chose to investigate whether psilocybin’s effects on brain rhythms are fixed or depend on the context of the individual. They recorded EEG data from 56 participants, once at baseline and once about 200 minutes after a 19 mg dose of psilocybin. Participants were tested across 4 conditions (eyes closed rest, guided meditation, music listening and movie viewing).


At rest, Psilocybin reduced alpha power in posterior regions, including default mode and visual areas, as well as lowering alpha coherence between nearly all network pairs. This was most apparent in salience, dorsal attention, frontoparietal and somatomotor networks. Researchers suggest this reflects a loosening of the structured long range coordination that normally constrains cortical activity, in line with the idea that psychedelics relax top-down control.


During meditation and music, no alpha connectivity reductions survived statistical correction, and during the movie, connectivity increased across networks. A statistical model that separated the effects of drug, context and their interaction showed that psilocybin alone lowered alpha connectivity, while its interaction with music and movie encouraged connectivity in the opposite direction with music had its strongest effect on limbic connectivity. Meditation had the weakest effect.


Based on this, researchers suggest brain activity under psilocybin reflects a competition between internally generated psychedelic dynamics, and incoming sensory input. When stimulation is low (such as during rest) the drug’s effects dominate. When the environment provides structured input, brain activity organises around the stimulus and the drug’s signature shrinks or reverses. Therefore, environment should be strongly considered as an effective part of the psychedelic experience, and not just as background.

There is growing evidence psychedelics show potential to treat psychiatric conditions involving dysfunctional fear processing such as PTSD. Most animal research uses the head twitch response (HTR) as a behavioural marker of psychedelic experiences, but some non-psychedelic drugs also trigger this response. Researchers therefore investigated whether psychedelics reduce innate fear by using a looming test. Here, mice see an expanding shadow that mimics an approaching predator and naturally freeze in response.


They compared a psychedelic compound, 4-AcO-DMT (psilacetin), with two forms of MDMA. All the drugs triggered head-twitching, but only psilacetin reduced freezing when the threat appeared. The MDMA-like drugs either left freezing unchanged or increased it. This suggests the looming test separates classical serotonergic psychedelics from related compounds better than the standard head-twitch test.


Researchers then found this response was mediated by the prelimbic region (PrL) by measuring levels of ERG1 (a protein that appears shortly after a neuron fires). When researchers lesioned this area of the brain, freezing was no longer shortened confirming the PrL is responsible for mediating this effect.


Researchers then identified that this effect was specifically mediated by the activity of CaMKIIα neurons. Permanently removing CaMKIIα neurons lowered freezing even in undrugged mice, and switching CaMKIIα neurons on alone reproduced the benefit without any drug. Additionally, when researchers deleted the 5-HT2a receptor gene specifically from PrL CaMKIIα neurons, the effect was abolished, confirming that 5-HT2a receptors (which psychedelics traditionally act on) are responsible for activating these cells in the PrL.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41380-026-03894-9

Some initial evidence has put psychedelics forward as a potential therapy for neuropathic pain. In this study researchers investigate the anti-allodynic properties of psilocybin in a neuropathic pain (NP) rat model, and elucidate psilocybin’s mechanism of action underlying effects on emotional aspects of pain.


Neuropathy was induced using Spinal Nerve Ligation (SNL) in Wistar rats. Fifteen days later, baseline mechanical allodynia was assessed using von Frey (VF) filaments. NP rats were then split into three experimental groups with allodynia being evaluated every 30 min for 5 hours using VF filaments following administration of saline or psilocybin.


In vivo single-unit electrophysiological recordings were taken in the rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM) to assess ON (pronociceptive) and OFF (antinociceptive) cells associated with nociceptive processing. Additionally, in vivo single-unit electrophysiological recordings were also conducted in glutamatergic neurons of the ACC, since the ACC has been implied in being associated with the emotional aspects of pain.


Psilocybin did not significantly reverse mechanical allodynia during the 0–5 h period. Despite this, a transient, non-significant reduction in allodynia was observed at 30 min and 1 h after receiving psilocybin.


Electrophysiological recordings from RVM neurons indicated that psilocybin did not affect the firing rate of ON-cells or OFF-cells. Whereas glutamatergic neurons in the ACC of NP rats exhibited a higher baseline firing rate to non-NP rats. In NP rats, psilocybin significantly decreased ACC glutamatergic activity relative to baseline.


Together, these results demonstrate that acute administration of psilocybin only temporarily (30 minutes- 1hr) reverses spinal nerve ligation-induced mechanical allodynia and has no effects in ON/OFF neurons. Additionally, psilocybin reduces and normalizes firing rate of excitatory glutamatergic neurons in the ACC, indicating that psilocybin’s effect on NP can be partially explained by its ability to modulate excitatory neurons in brain areas associated with emotional aspects of pain.

August 2026

Volume 8 · 1 Paper1 ·

Researchers sought to investigate how psilocybin alters visual perception by examining its effects on the brain’s ability to distinguish between expected and unexpected visual information. Specifically, they investigated how psilocybin affects neuronal activity in the visual cortex, with particular interest in somatostatin (SST) interneurons, which have previously been implicated in processing prediction errors and unexpected sensory information.


To investigate this, mice were trained to recognise a sequence of eight images and lick a waterspout when an image unexpectedly changed. Following psilocybin administration, mice showed a dramatic reduction in their ability to detect these changes, with performance falling to approximately chance levels. Importa ntly, this was not accompanied by any detectable changes in general motor behaviour, suggesting the impairment was more likely related to altered visual processing than an inability to perform the task.


When examining neuronal activity, researchers identified a distinctive 4-Hz oscillation in response to visual stimuli following psilocybin administration. This effect was particularly pronounced in SST interneurons within visual cortical regions. Interestingly, this oscillatory activity was largely absent in frontal cortical regions, suggesting psilocybin's effects on neuronal activity are region-specific.


Researchers then investigated whether psilocybin affected the brain's ability to distinguish between image identity and image changes. They found that the representation of image identity remained largely unaffected. However, neurons responsible for detecting changes became significantly more active, even when mice were repeatedly presented with an unchanged image. This effect was strongest in SST interneurons, suggesting that psilocybin causes the brain to interpret familiar visual information as though something unexpected has occurred.


Further investigation revealed that psilocybin also temporarily shifted overall patterns of neuronal activity towards those normally associated with image changes. This suggests that psilocybin generates an internal signal resembling sensory surprise, even when the visual environment remains unchanged.


Interestingly, the strength of these effects depended on the visual characteristics of the images themselves. Images containing smoother, more continuous contours produced stronger oscillatory responses than images with fragmented contours.


Together, these findings suggest that psilocybin disrupts the normal distinction between expected and unexpected visual information, potentially explaining some of the visual distortions experienced during psychedelic states.

July 2026

Volume 7 · 3 Papers ·

While both psilocybin and MDMA are psychedelics, MDMA belongs to the class of entactogens, therefore working by releasing serotonin (5HT) as well as noradrenaline (NE) and Dopamine (DA). Psilocybin on the other hand, interacts with 5-HT2a receptors to produce psychedelic experiences in humans and head twitch responses (HTR) in rodents. However, MDMA does not produce HTR to the same extent. Given this, researchers proposed that the NE release produced by MDMA acts on a2 adrenergic receptors (a2R) to suppress the 5-HT2a mediated behaviour of HTR’s


Behaviourally, psilocybin and 2C-B (both serotonergic psychedelics) produced strong HTR, while MDMA, methylone, and MBDB (all entactogens), were statistically indistinguishable from saline, with MDMA and methylone reducing HTR at higher doses.


When examining chemical changes in the brain, researchers found. all entactogens raised both 5HT and NE levels in the mPFC, while psilocybin changed neither. To test the effect of specifically NE on HTR behaviour, researchers gave mice reboxetine to blunt MDMA’s NE release. When mice were given MDMA + reboxetine, there was a large increase in HTR. When reboxetine + psilocybin were given together, there was an increase in NE compared to just psilocybin treatment, which produced a reduced amount of HTR in mice. Together these findings suggest NE is responsible for blocking HTR behaviour in normal MDMA treatment. Additional tests using an a2R agonist reveal that this effect depended specifically on the activation of the a2R.


Researchers also found that the activation of a2 adrenergic receptors did not inhibit psilocybin’s expected antidepressant effect. This is important since researchers suggested that increases in NE to reduce HTR behaviours present the possibility of reducing unnecessary side effects of psychedelics that may disrupt its ability to be used as a therapeutic tool.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.10.737743

Neural avalanches are brief bursts of coordinated brain activity, that are traditionally looked at in terms their size and duration, however treating avalanches in this way hides what they actually look like. Whether recurring shapes exist among avalanches has not been systematically studied, mainly because avalanches vary in duration and spatial extent, making them hard to compare. The researchers argue that starting from discrete events rather than breaking down a whole recording into brain wide patterns avoids blurring brief episodes of coordinated activity with the unstructured activity between them.


This is relevant to the activity of psychedelics, since they are often thought to increase entropy in the brain, meaning neural activity is more random and less predictable. Therefore, by tracking recurring avalanche patterns, the researchers are measuring the ways in which psilocybin supresses or encourages these patterns.


The researchers built a distance measure that compares avalanches of different lengths and spatial layouts. This was then applied to EEG recording at baseline and 75 to 120 minutes after psilocybin treatment. Hierarchical clustering grouped the avalanches into 12 recurring patterns. These patterns were then searched for in the continuous, unthresholded data, and runs where the same pattern recurred at least twice in immediate succession were classified as either oscillating or stable.


Within the 12 patterns that formed, some visibly moved across the scalp (travelling), while others kept a fixed layout (standing). Clusters did not differ significantly in how often they arose between baseline and psilocybin sessions, suggesting the patterns themselves appear to exist in both states. Many patterns were mirrored across the two hemispheres, with each pair sharing the same temporal behaviour.


Patterns with visible spatial movement (0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 10) were almost exclusively oscillating, whereas patterns with fixed layouts (7, 9, 11) were almost exclusively stable. Critically, under psilocybin, the proportion of oscillating sequences fell from 85.7% to 65.2%, while the stable proportion rose from 14.3% to 34.8%, thus creating a shift towards more stable pattern of activity. The researchers identify two contributors to this. Firstly, oscillating travelling clusters were much less active under psilocybin, with clusters 2 and 5 contributing most. Secondly, cluster 8 changed its own from 31% stable at baseline to 71% stable under psilocybin.


These findings suggest that psilocybin does not increase entropy in the brain. It instead selectively reduces rhythmic, alpha range travelling patterns across the back of the head, shifting the balance toward spatially fixed ones. The researchers present this as a more nuanced account of how neural dynamics change.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.10.737743

Previous work has suggested that psilocybin may be able to promote cognitive flexibility, however little is known about what neural changes may underlie this change in cognition. To investigate this, researchers recorded single neurons in the rat medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) during a probabilistic choice task.


8 Rats were given a probabilistic choice task, where reward likelihood shifted across blocks. This produced a high uncertainty block with 50/50 reward likelihood, as well as 2 certain blocks with 80/20 and 20/80 reward likelihoods. Rats were treated with psilocybin 15 minutes before testing, and results were compared to their own behaviour from a previous session.


In rats given psilocybin, they found that although strategies largely stayed the same, there was an increase in choice variability in high uncertainty trials but not low uncertainty ones. Researchers suggest this may reflect an increase in adaptive exploration. To examine this effect further, computational models we tested with the winning model having 4 relevant parameters. 2 were responsible for modulating update rates following rewarded and unrewarded outcomes, a bias parameter capturing value independent preference for one side, and an inverse temperature parameter capturing the degree to which choices were driven by learned values.


Psilocybin did not affect the temperature parameter but moderately decreased the bias parameter. More importantly, psilocybin increased the learning rate for rewarded outcomes and strongly decreased the learning rate for unrewarded outcomes. This suggests psilocybin modulates decision making behaviour by reweighting how rewarded versus unrewarded outcomes update choice values, as well as reducing value independent bias.


When analysing neural behaviour 5 clusters were identified. 2 clusters of neurons showed increases in activity associated with reward, with distinct temporal patterns emerging between them. Type 1 showed reward-related increases immediately at action and type 2 later during the outcome. Psilocybin increased the proportion of type 1 but not 2 neurons in the population. Another 2 clusters of neurons showed the same thing but for unrewarded outcomes with similar distinct temporal patterns. In unrewarded clusters psilocybin selectively reduced representation of the Type I neurons, suggesting psilocybin enhances representation of rewarded actions and attenuates representation of unrewarded actions.


Overall, researchers suggest psilocybin reweights learning outcomes, favouring learning from rewards, with this behavioural change being mirrored by the behaviour of neurons in the mPFC. They note this is consistent with evidence suggesting psilocybin relaxes priors, with decreased choice related coding allowing a “freer” landscape for choice under uncertainty.

June 2026

Volume 6 · 3 Papers ·

Psychedelics are traditionally thought to alter activity in the default mode network (DMN) with the DMN at the top of the cortex’s hierarchy. Some evidence suggests psychedelics amplify bottom-up activity flowing into the DMN by relaxing top-down priors, while other findings suggest they weaken bottom-up activity.


researchers here argue this inconsistency is unresolved since most imaging studies treat activity as fixed in space rather than tracking how it moves. Additionally, cortical signalling is mostly local, but many studies compare distant region pairs. Researchers therefore decided to use an optical flow technique, this tracks frame to frame movement of activity across the cortical surface to measure the magnitude and direction of activity movement within the DMN.


9 drug to placebo comparisons across 4 studies were pooled, including the use of LSD, MDMA and psilocybin which in this paper are considered together under the umbrella of ‘psychedelics.


Psychedelics reduced the magnitude of propagations in the DMN in every comparison, while Diazepam and dexmedetomidine instead increased magnitude. Bottom-up directionality was also reduced by MDMA, LSD. Diazepam reduced bottom-up flow less than LSD, while dexmedetomidine increased it. Effects were strongest in the posterior DMN, and much stronger in mice.


Based on these findings, researchers suggest psychedelics reduce bottom-up propagation into the DMN consistently across compounds. This supports theories that suggest psychedelics shift the balance toward top-down processing, rather than amplifying bottom-up flow.


Because reduced bottom-up flow tracked negative experiences, they speculate that over disrupting a normal balance may cause distress. More speculatively, they suggest this may help ruminative depression, where bottom-up activity may be excessive, while posing risk for people prone to psychosis.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2522000123

While studies that use EEG measurements to investigate psychedelics typically examine changes in spectral features or signal complexity, minimal attention has been given to microstate analysis approaches within psychedelic literature. 4 different microstates appear to account for the majority of variance in resting state EEG; however, altered distribution and transition patterns, and reduced duration of these microstates have been reported across a variety of psychiatric conditions.


Given the significant changes in experience during psychedelic experiences, researchers hypothesised that there would be modulation in microstates present during the acute phase of psilocybin but also examined whether individual differences in psychedelic microstates were able to predict long term psychological changes a month after the experience.


The most predictable finding was further evidence that psilocybin increases diversity in neural dynamics. Increases in global field power peaks line up with existing literature explaining psychedelic experiences as at least in part being caused by increases in entropy across the brain.


Since microstate analysis in psychedelic research is fairly new, researchers chose to test cross-validation measurements used to determine the appropriate number of microstates. Interestingly, they found that 3 microstates more appropriately explained their data and so continued their analysis using both traditional 4 microstate analysis as well as 3 microstate analysis.


The strongest finding was a significant reduction in microstate lifespan under psilocybin, particularly during peak intoxication. This suggests that large scale brain states become less temporally stable, with the brain transitioning between different configurations more rapidly. Correspondingly, the frequency of microstate occurrence increased during peak intoxication, supporting the interpretation that psilocybin accelerates the rate at which the brain moves between states. These changes broadly followed the time course of psilocin concentration and subjective intoxication.


Despite these temporal changes, microstate coverage remained largely unchanged, suggesting that psilocybin alters the length and transition frequency of these states, rather than changing which states are accessible. Interestingly, individual differences in microstate dynamics were associated with the intensity of subjective psychedelic experiences, as well as positive psychological changes reported 28 days later. Less stable microstate characterisation (shorter with more frequent change) was associated with more intense acute experiences and greater positive persisting effects. It should be noted these relationships remain exploratory and do not establish a causal relationship between neural dynamics and subsequent psychological change.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.09.731183

PTSD is often associated with dysfunction in the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Prior research has implicated psilocybin as increasing neuroplasticity in different regions in the brain as part of its antidepressant effect, with other research showing psilocybin can help fear extinction. Given this, researchers were interested in examining how long psilocybin’s effects on cued fear memory last, while examining what neural changes may underlie this. The researchers hypothesized that psilocybin reduces fear memory by promoting neuroplasticity in these two regions.


Male mice were split into three groups of 16 where 2 groups were fear conditioned either with or without psilocybin, and another control group that received no fear conditioning. 2 days before psilocybin or saline administration, mice in fear condition groups had 5 sound tones paired mild foot shock. Responses to the fear tones were measured 1 and 6 days after psilocybin / saline administration in a new testing chamber, and 7 days later in the original chamber. Brain tissue was later analysed to examine differences in neuroplasticity across groups.


As expected, fear conditioned mice froze far more than controls, with psilocybin reducing freezing on day 1, 6 and 7 compared with mice given saline. Mice exposed to fear conditioning showed reduced dendritic branching and spine density (both markers of reduced neuroplasticity) in the hippocampus and mPFC, with psilocybin reversing this effect in both regions. Fear conditioning also lowered BDNF, mTOR, GluR1 and synapsin-1 levels in both regions, with psilocybin again raised all of them back. Finally, fear conditioning reduced DCX-positive cells in the dentate gyrus, with psilocybin restoring them to near control levels.


Researchers interoperated all these findings as an indicator that a single dose of psilocybin can rapidly and continuously reduce fear memory for up 7 days. This was associated with restored structural plasticity, activation of the BDNF-mTOR pathway, increased plasticity related proteins, and greater neurogenesis. The researchers suggest that psilocybin reduces fear memory at least in part by restoring losses in neuroplasticity, with psilocybin having potential for treating PTSD and other disorders involving fear memory.

April 2026

Volume 5 · 2 Papers ·

Researchers investigated astrocytes’ contribution to the rapid antidepressant effects of psilocybin using a chronic restraint stress (CRS) mouse model. Psilocybin results were also compared to ketamine, (a drug with concrete rapid acting antidepressant effects). Mice were subjected to 28 days of restraint stress in order to develop depressive symptoms. A single dose of psilocybin or ketamine was able to reverse these effects within 1 to 3 days, thus confirming both drugs' rapid antidepressant efficacy.


To investigate the involvement of astrocyte function in this effect, researchers measured GFAP (a biomarker of astrocytes) and C3 (a marker of neurotoxic A1 reactive astrocytes) in the prefrontal cortex. After the SRC procedure, mice showed reduced GFAP markers and increased C3 expression. This suggested both astrocyte loss as well as a shift toward harmful A1 astrocyte phenotype. Following psilocybin and ketamine treatment, both of these changes in biomarker levels were restored indicating healthier astrocyte behaviour after mice received these treatments.


In cultured astrocytes, psilocin treatment showed an increase in astrocyte proliferation and enhanced release of ATP, lactate and glutamate, all gliotransmitters linked to antidepressant activity. When astrocytes were converted into the toxic A1 phenotype using inflammatory cytokines, they showed impaired gliotransmitter release and mitochondrial dysfunction. After psilocin treatment, these deficits were reversed, producing reduced C3 expression and restoring ATP, lactate, and glutamate release as well as healthier mitochondrial activity.


Researchers depleted roughly half of the astrocytes in the prelimbic prefrontal cortex of unstressed mice with the use of a genetically modified virus. This partial depletion alone did not produce depressive or anxious behaviour, but blunted psilocybin's anti-anxiety and antidepressant effects across multiple testing days. Thus suggesting astrocytes are functionally necessary for psilocybin's rapid action.


DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pnpbp.2026.111698

Due to the growing body of research demonstrating Psilocybin’s antidepressant potential, researchers chose to investigate psilocybin’s modulatory effect on 3 major types of GABAergic neurons in the mouse medial frontal cortex. These were somatostatin (SST), parvalbumin and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) interneurons.


As expected, mice given saline did not show any change in interneuron firing activity. However, mice given psilocybin showed different cell specific changes. SST neurons decreased firing rates, where as PV neurons showed the opposite and increased firing rates. The majority of recorded SST and PV interneurons located in were layer 5, meaning psilocybin shows an opposing effect on 2 different GABA interneuron populations.


Researchers were particularly interested in the mechanism behind the reduction in SST interneuron firing. This is because this reduction in signalling should create a disinhibitory effect which could be potential mechanism explaining increases in plasticity associated with psilocybin treatment.


To investigate the possible mechanism behind the reduction in SST signalling, researchers first examined changes in VIP interneurons, since VIP interneurons are known to supress the activity of SST interneurons. When imaging VIP interneurons, researchers found no difference in activity, before and after psilocybin was administered thus ruling out the potential for this indirect mechanism.


Researchers then investigated the relevance of 5-HT1a receptors, since these are known to be expressed on SST interneurons, and are known to dampen the excitability of neurons. To test this, researchers blocked the 5-HT1a receptor pharmacologically, and separately genetically knocked out this receptor from SST interneurons. In both cases, psilocybin no longer had a suppressive effect on SST signalling thus confirming that 5-HT1a receptors are responsible for psilocybin’s effect on SST interneuron signal suppression.


Behaviourally, mice without 5-HT1a receptors on SST interneurons did not show the expected antidepressant like effects that would typically be expected after psilocybin administration. They did however show head twitch responses, which is a classic marker of an acute psychedelic experience. This suggests the reduction in 5-HT1a mediated SST interneuron signalling is likely responsible for specifically longer-term antidepressant effects of psilocybin.

The claustrum is a small subcortical structure sitting between the insular cortex and striatum, densely packed with serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. Despite long being theorised as a hub for sensory integration and consciousness, how psychedelics actually alter its neurochemistry had never been directly examined.


This study used in vivo microdialysis in freely moving rats to measure how psilocin and the synthetic compound 25I-NBOMe altered extracellular neurotransmitter levels directly within the claustrum. Both compounds showed elevated 5-HT (serotonin) levels, but 25I-NBOMe produced the strongest cumulative response.
